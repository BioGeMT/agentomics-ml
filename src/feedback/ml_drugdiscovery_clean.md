## Machine learning in preclinical drug discovery
Drug-discovery and drug-development endeavors are laborious, costly and time consuming. These programs can take upward of 12 years and cost US $2.5 billion, with a failure rate of more than 90%. Machine learning (ML) presents an opportunity to improve the drug-discovery process. Indeed, with the growing abundance of public and private large-scale biological and chemical datasets, ML techniques are becoming well positioned as useful tools that can augment the traditional drug-development process. In this Perspective, we discuss the integration of algorithmic methods throughout the preclinical phases of drug discovery. Specifically, we highlight an array of ML-based efforts, across diverse disease areas, to accelerate initial hit discovery, mechanism-of-action (MOA) elucidation and chemical property optimization. With advances in the application of ML across diverse therapeutic areas, we posit that fully ML-integrated drug-discovery pipelines will define the future of drug-development programs.

Drug discovery is a prolonged and failure-prone process that requires a substantial investment of financial capital and expert human resources. Novel drug discovery and development can cost more than US $2.5 billion and can require 12-15 years of sustained effort. For academic institutions and pharmaceutical companies, the experimental pipeline is common: following biological target identification and validation, chemical inhibitors are typically (but not always) found through high-throughput screening (HTS) of chemical libraries. Hit compounds from these chemical screens are further investigated for MOA elucidation, structure-activity relationships and in vivo efficacy. However, this traditional approach is associated with a roughly 90% failure rate for candidate compounds traversing from phase 1 clinical trials to market approval, with a much higher rate when accounting for the preclinical stages of discovery. Indeed, clinical trial data suggest that the steep failure rate, even at later stages of development, can be attributed to a lack of commercial needs and poor strategic planning (approximately 10%), insufficient drug-like properties (approximately 15%), unacceptable toxicity (approximately 30%) and lack of clinical efficacy (approximately 50%). As such, there is an urgent and largely unmet need to increase the efficiency as well as to decrease the cost of the drug-discovery and drug-development process.

In the past couple of decades, there has been a remarkable adoption of artificial intelligence (AI) techniques to help address pressing real-world challenges. This was made possible through the development of a wide array of ML technologies that are directly applicable to diverse data types. Generally, these algorithms use statistical techniques that learn from input datasets to make predictions about new data, without coding explicit rules. Importantly, the performance of these approaches inherently improves as the quality and quantity of training data increase. Therefore, ML methods are ideally suited for solving problems involving large datasets with many variables connected by non-obvious and complex relationships, which is often the case in drug-discovery and drug-development efforts. Indeed, with the increasing accumulation of sophisticated chemical and biological datasets, ML techniques are uniquely positioned to maximally leverage these datasets as a means of more completely automating the drug-discovery pipeline, particularly at the preclinical stages where training data are currently most abundant.

Excitingly, academic laboratories and pharmaceutical companies are more commonly implementing ML techniques that are being applied at each of the preclinical stages of drug development. Consequently, these groups have also invested substantial resources into the generation of datasets that support ML-integrated drug-discovery approaches.  In this Perspective, we outline the application of various ML approaches throughout preclinical drug discovery of small-molecule therapeutics for an array of disease areas. We divide the Perspective into three drug-discovery steps:

- Step 1: hit identification.
- Step 2: MOA elucidation.
- Step 3: translational investigations.

Our goal is to highlight the growing impact of ML in drug discovery and catalyze its broad application toward the development of end-to-end automated drug-discovery pipelines across diverse therapeutic areas.

## Step 1: hit identification

## Virtual screening

HTS, which tests large compound sets against target proteins or whole-cell cultures, has been a critical avenue for drug discovery. However, it is costly and time intensive, often taking weeks or months to screen 10 5 to 10 6 compounds and only finding a few with therapeutic potential. ML-guided virtual screening offers a promising alternative by using ML models to rapidly evaluate compounds in silico, enabling the efficient exploration of chemical libraries orders of magnitude larger than what can be explored using HTS.

Molecular featurization in virtual screening . More specifically, the emergence of deep quantitative structure-activity relationship models (using deep learning models for quantifying structure-activity relationships) has improved virtual screening of large chemical libra  ries. Notably, deep learning has had a substantial impact in molecular featurization 16 . Traditionally, molecules were presented to ML algorithms as fixed representations, such as fingerprint vectors that denote the presence or absence of mole  cular sub  structures 16,20,21 . These representations, however, often fail to capture the contextual details of molecular connectivity and cannot be adapted during model training to better suit the prediction task at hand.

To incorporate task- and context-aware molecular embeddings, previous works have applied a directed message-passing neural network (D-MPNN) for novel antibiotic discovery for Escherichia coli and Acinetobacter baumannii. D-MPNNs work by initializing each node (atom) and edge (bond) in a molecular graph with a vector and subsequently updating these vectors based on surrounding chemistry through a series of message-passing steps. In both studies, the models were trained  on  binarized  growthinhibition data against the pathogens of interest. The models were then used to make predictions on the Drug Repurposing Hub, a collection 24 of around 7,000 compounds with favorable toxicity and pharmacokinetic profiles. This led to the discoveries of halicin ( E. coli minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) ≈ 2 μg ml -1 ) and abaucin ( A. baumannii MIC ≈ 2 μg ml -1 ), respectively. Notably, both studies show that prioritization of top-scoring molecules can substantially increase in vitro hit rates when compared with traditional HTS approaches.

Recently, this D-MPNN was also used to discover new antibacterial molecules by leveraging a tree search algorithm to identify antibiotic 'rationales' (that is, core substructures that drive antibacterial predictions). Two compounds with a common substructure showed activity against Staphylococcus aureus . It is important to note, however, that using a model that was trained to predict the activity of entire compounds for the identification of substructures could lead to inaccuracies in identifying smaller scaffolds. Indeed, in this study, the identified rationales constitute nearly the entire molecule. Furthermore, labeling this approach as 'explainable' remains a point of discussion. Although it offers some understanding of the model's predictions, it remains unclear why a novel rationale is being identified by the D-MPNN.

Structure-based virtual screening . Rather than relying on phenotypic chemical screens as training data, protein-ligand docking can be leveraged to screen compounds based on their predicted binding affinity to a target protein. However, when applied to ultra-large chemical libraries, physics-based docking algorithms that aim to dock each molecule onto the protein of interest are extremely time consuming, requiring substantial computational resources (often &gt;1,000 CPU and GPU). As a result, Deep Docking was developed to enhance docking-based virtual screening for chemical libraries of over a billion molecules, integrating traditional docking techniques with deep learning to accelerate the virtual screening process(Fig. 2b). Initially, Deep Docking takes a small subset (approximately 1%) of molecules from the virtual library and docks them using conventional methods. These docking scores are used to train a feedforward neural network (FFNN), which can be used to rapidly predict docking scores for the remaining molecules (approximately 99%) in the chemical library. As a concrete example, Deep Docking was used to screen 1.3 billion compounds in the ZINC15 database against the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) main protease (M pro ). Subsequent laboratory validation resulted in the identification of a new class of compounds targeting M pro. The use of docking-based virtual screening still comes with limitations. For instance, property labels that are used to train the model are generated using in silico docking algorithms, which come with varying levels of accuracy based on the structure of the ligand and protein as well as the parametrization of the docking software. Indeed, the lack of ground truth labels can introduce error into downstream predictions with the model.

## Molecular generation

Beyond virtual screening of in silico chemical libraries, the chemical search space for bioactive molecules can be further expanded through generative deep learning approaches. For reference, the upper size limit of chemical libraries that can be reasonably virtually screened lies on the order of billions, a number that is dwarfed by drug-like chemical space, which is estimated to be upward of 10 60 molecules. Indeed, generative models enable the exploration of uncharted regions of chemical space that have yet to be captured by current open-source chemical libraries.

Variational autoencoders . Variational autoencoders (VAEs) have been widely used in molecular design tasks since a 2018 landmark study demonstrated their effectiveness. In brief, VAEs consist of two neural networks: an encoder and a decoder. During training, the encoder learns to compress a molecule into a continuous and low-dimensional embedding (latent space) while the decoder learns to reconstruct the molecule from this latent space. During sampling, the encoder is unnecessary, allowing the decoder to freely sample from the latent space. Importantly, this process can be controlled such that the decoder systematically targets regions in the latent space that enhance the probability of generating novel molecules with desired properties.

Much of the early research using VAEs concentrated on decoding molecules as simplified input line-entry system (SMILES) strings. In 2019, junction tree VAE (JT-VAE) improved upon these studies by working directly with molecular graph structures instead of SMILES strings. Investigators suggested that working with graph structures directly improves the model's ability to discern structural relationships within and between molecules. To depict molecules as graphs, they first converted standard molecular graphs into junction trees consisting of branches that connect individual building block elements (bonds and rings, for example). These junction trees, along with the original molecular graph, then underwent encoding and decoding processes concurrently. This approach yielded 100% valid molecular structures and surpassed existing VAE models in both unconstrained and constrained molecular optimization tasks, highlighting the utility of graph-based generative approaches.

Subsequent applications of VAE-based models have shown their utility in preclinical drug design. For instance, JAEGER, a JT-VAE-based model, was leveraged to design novel antimalarial compounds. The authors integrated a property-prediction component with the JT-VAE model, jointly training it for molecular encoding and decoding and antimalarial activity optimization (negative logarithm of the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (pIC 50) in a malaria proliferation assay). During sampling, their model iteratively explored the latent space for novel molecules, retaining only those surpassing a certain predicted activity threshold, yielding two molecules that exhibited nanomolar activity against the 3D7 strain of Plasmodium falciparum and low hepatocellular cytotoxicity.

VAEs were also leveraged to identify a potent DDR1 kinase inhibitor. The generated compounds demonstrated nanomolar inhibition of DDR1, metabolic stability in vitro and promising pharmacokinetics in a mouse model. It is, however, important to note that the most potent compound from this work is structurally similar and roughly equipotent to ponatinib, an already marketed kinase inhibitor, raising some discussion about the true utility of the model.

Normalizing flow and diffusion models. Recently, the application of normalizing flow and diffusion models in de novo drug design has gained traction, owing to their achievements in other domains such as image and text generation. Like VAEs, normalizing flow models define transformations from a complex distribution (that is, observable molecules) to simpler distributions and vice versa. The fundamental distinction lies in that normalizing flow models use invertible transformations between simple distributions (latent space) and complex distributions (observational space), facilitating the exact computation of sample likelihood to assess the quality of the generated data. Conversely, VAEs rely on an approximate likelihood, introducing imprecision into the generative process.

GraphAF, for example, is a flow-based model that generates novel molecules in an autoregressive manner, producing new nodes and connecting them with edges at each time step based on the current subgraph. GraphAF also incorporates reinforcement learning into its autoregressive generation process, rewarding each step based on specific properties (such as the octanol-water coefficient (log( P )) and quantitative estimate of drug-likeness (QED)). GraphAF displays competitive performance with JT-VAE, a SMILES-based recurrent neural network (RNN) generator, and graph-based reinforcement learning techniques in generating unique and valid molecules and outperforms these models in optimizing log( P ) and QED.

Diffusion models operate in a slightly different manner. First, a noising process (forward diffusion) iteratively adds Gaussian noise to a sample in a fixed nontrainable manner. Subsequently, the model is trained to denoise the sample (reverse diffusion) in a stepwise manner, such that small portions of noise are removed until the original sample is restored. This stepwise denoising process provides an advantage over VAEs and generative adversarial networks that transform latent space to a sample in a single decoding step. The iterative nature of diffusion models provides an opportunity to gradually improve a sample, resulting in a more refined sample.

In 2022, a novel equivariant diffusion model was developed for generating novel molecules in three-dimensional (3D) space. Here, molecules were represented as a collection of atom features and coordinates, indicating their position in 3D space. During the diffusion process, noise was added and removed in a stepwise manner, initially corrupting the molecule and later restoring it to its original form. Importantly, the reverse diffusion process could be conditioned such that the resulting molecules could be optimized for some property of interest. The authors highlight their model's ability to incorporate property information when creating 3D molecules that satisfy physical chemistry objectives such as polarizability and heat capacity. Moreover, it outperforms prior VAE and flow-based models in generating both valid and unique molecules. However, when tested on a dataset with larger, drug-like compounds, the model has difficulty producing stable molecules, suggesting a limited application to design of new drugs.

Chemical language models . String-based molecular representations, such as SMILES, have made it possible to leverage chemical language models (CLMs) for de novo molecular design. Commonly, CLMs apply RNNs, which work on sequential data by conditioning new outputs on previously seen sequence elements. CLMs can be trained in a self-supervised manner on extensive collections of molecular strings with desirable properties (known bioactive compounds, for example), aiming to identify and learn the common token patterns that characterize the training sequences.

Once trained, novel compounds that closely mirror the physicochemical property distributions observed in the training set can be generated autoregressively. Conventionally, a major pitfall with CLMs was the requirement of large training sets, on the order of hundreds of thousands to millions of compounds, to reliably learn (1) valid molecular string syntax and (2) the associated physicochemical properties. Considering the data scarceness of many therapeutic areas, this limited the application of CLMs in drug design.

To overcome this, transfer learning has been used in the design of novel lead compounds. Here, a model is pretrained on a related task for which data are not limited (large drug-like libraries, for example). Subsequently, initial layers in a neural network can be frozen to maintain information from pretraining, and terminal layers are fine-tuned on data specific to the desired task for which data are limited. This biases the generation process toward the latter task. This approach was used to generate novel ligands for PI3Kγ, an anticancer, antiinflammatory and immunomodulatory drug target. Here, the authors pretrained a long short-term memory neural network, which is a type of RNN, on approximately 850,000 SMILES codes from the US patent database and fine-tuned it on 46 known PI3Kγ inhibitors. After generating over 1 million novel SMILES strings, they used a classifier model to prioritize them for synthesis. Among the generated molecules, they selected two for synthesis that were predicted with high confidence by the classifier as well as four structural analogs. All six compounds exhibited nanomolar activity against PI3Kγ in vitro.

The natural language-processing field saw a major shift after the release of the transformer model. Transformer models offer advantages over RNN-based models by enabling parallel processing for accelerated training, capturing longer-term dependencies in sequences and providing a degree of interpretability through attention layers. MolGPT, based on the generative pretraining transformer (GPT) model, was the first study leveraging this type of model in sequence-modeling tasks. Compared with other generative models, MolGPT displays competitive performance in several benchmarks such as uniqueness, validity and novelty. Moreover, to guide the generated molecules toward a particular molecular property or scaffold, MolGPT passes a condition vector containing properties of interest (for example, calculated log( P ) (clog( P ))) or the desired scaffold, alongside the start token, enabling it to generate new SMILES codes that satisfy these conditions. Indeed, MolGPT displays good performance in both single- and multi-property optimization tasks as well as scaffold-constrained generation tasks. Importantly, MolGPT showcases the interpretability of transformers through saliency maps, highlighting which SMILES tokens carry greater weight in the model's decision process 54 . This interpretability is vital for instilling trust in the model's capacity to learn chemically and biologically relevant features.

Transformer-based large language models (LLMs) are also being increasingly applied for generative chemistry. For instance, it was shown that, through simple text prompts asking the model to generate compounds with some property, a GPT3-based LLM could generate structurally novel molecules that satisfied that property. Indeed, several other studies have shown the potential of transformers and LLMs in this space. However, their adoption is still young, and there has yet to be any studies developing concrete preclinical leads with real-world validation.

## Step 2: MOA elucidation

After hit identification, a detailed mechanistic understanding of the biological function of a compound is essential. In the context of cell-based screening, MOA elucidation requires thorough and orthogonal approaches, including genetic methods such as genome-scale chemical genetic screening and direct biochemical methods. These types of laboratory methods are typically low throughput and resource intensive. Alternatively, integrating deep learning techniques with existing large-scale databases, such as the Protein Data Bank (PDB), can help determine the functions of novel bioactive molecules. This approach is likely to streamline MOA elucidation and enhance structure-based molecular optimization.

## Multiple-sequence alignment-based protein structure prediction

AlphaFold (AF2) was a breakthrough in predicting 3D structures of proteins from primary amino acid sequences. In the 14th biennial Critical Assessment of Structure Prediction challenge, DeepMind's AF2 achieved the best predictive modeling accuracy. Although both homo logy modeling and AF2 use multiple-sequence alignment (MSA), AF2 uniquely leverages coevolutionary information from sequences that are homologous to the protein of interest. AF2 takes the input sequence for the protein of interest and generates MSA and pair representations. Here, the MSA matrix is an alignment of the input sequence to homolog sequences, while the pair representation is a pairwise distance matrix between amino acids of the known 3D structure of homologs. These representations are inputted to Evoformer, a two-track neural network, which jointly embeds the spatial and evolutionary relationships between residues. Finally, the structure module generates a predicted 3D structure using backbone frames as well as the updated MSA vector and pair representation. The backbone frame is an additional input that represents each residue as an equivariant triangle with nodes corresponding to the Cα, the C atom of the carboxyl group and the N atom of the amino group. Using these three inputs, the structure module predicts relative rotations and translations of each backbone frame to obtain the 3D positions of all atoms in the protein.

Importantly, the robustness of AF2 is evidenced by its ability to generate accurate 3D models in cases of under-represented examples during training, such as proteins that selectively fold in the presence of an unknown molecule or intertwined homomers. However, the model poorly predicts for proteins with few homotypic or intrachain contacts relative to the number of heterotypic interactions. Generally, this occurs in large protein complexes with bridging domains, the shape of which is determined by interactions with other chains in the complex. Nonetheless, AF2 has already demonstrated utility to the structural biology community 63,64 and in drug discovery. For example, previous work used PandaOmics, an omics-based deep learning model, to shortlist liver cancer therapeutic candidates for which structures can be predicted by AF2. Here, small molecules were generated using Chemistry42, based on the AF2-predicted structures, for experimental validation. This approach enabled the discovery of a small molecule with activity against cyclin-dependent kinase 20 (CDK20), which is often overexpressed in many tumors, with a K d of 8.9 ± 1.6 μM. Indeed, these works stand as proofs of concept of the potential utility of AF2, thereby warranting further exploration of its application toward AF2-enabled structure-based drug design. However, given its known limitations, there certainly exist cases for which AF2 models will be insufficiently accurate to enhance structure-based drug design and/or optimization.

Shortly  after  AF2,  RoseTTAFold was  released  with  the goal of improving protein structural prediction accuracy. This model extends the two-track neural network 62 by adding a third track that accounts for 3D coordinates of the Cα, C and N atoms for each residue using an SE(3) (special Euclidean group in three dimensions)-transformer. Unlike AF2, where the 3D structure is elucidated after complete processing of the 1D amino acid sequence (MSA representation) and 2D distance matrices between amino acids (pair representation), RoseTTAFold simultaneously incorporates information from the 1D, 2D and 3D (backbone frames) representations to generate structure predictions . The investigators trained the model on more than 200,000 proteins with known structures from the PDB database 70 . To test the model's ability to provide insight into the function of human proteins under a disease state, they predicted the structure of a disintegrin and metalloprotease (ADAM) and its family of proteins, which mediate cell-cell and cell-matrix interactions; these proteins are involved in a wide array of human illnesses, such as neurological diseases and cancer metastasis. RoseTTAFold successfully predicted the structure of the ADAM33 prodomain, which was previously unknown and had no homologs of known structure. This prediction, containing a lipocalin-like β-barrel fold followed by an extension containing a cysteine, aligns with experimental data suggesting that the ADAM prodomain inhibits metalloprotease activity using a cysteine switch.

## Protein language model-based protein structure prediction

AF2 and RoseTTAFold leverage extensive evolutionary data obtained from MSAs, a resource not available for some proteins, such as those that evolve rapidly or orphan proteins. MSAs for rapidly evolving proteins, including antibody complementarity-determining regions, are typically noisy. Orphan proteins, by contrast, lack the necessary structural and sequence homology for reliable predictions based on MSAs. Protein language models (PLMs) may overcome this challenge by pretraining transformer-based models on protein sequences, which can learn patterns reflective of evolution while capturing secondary and tertiary structures. For example, OmegaFold uses OmegaPLM, a transformer-based PLM, pretrained on UniRef50 to learn singleand pairwise-residue (a matrix of interactions between two residues) representations. These representations are inputted into GeoFormer, a geometry-inspired transformer, to learn consistent structural and physical relationships between amino acids when projected into 3D space. The model's performance in predicting recently published antibody and orphan protein structures from the PDB was assessed. For both orphan proteins and antibody CDRs, OmegaFold achieved significantly higher prediction accuracy than AF2.

Similarly, ESMFold leverages ESM-2, an encoder-only transformer, trained on roughly 65 million unique protein sequences from UniRef. The single (masked amino acid sequence) and pairwise (a pairwise distance matrix between amino acids) embeddings generated by ESM-2 serve as the input for the folding trunk. This is a simplified adaptation of Evoformer that removes the MSA dependence to produce updated representations that are fed into the final structure module that predicts a 3D protein structure. Although ESMFold does not meet the accuracy of AF2, the model can generate accurate structure predictions for orphan proteins and is more than an order of magnitude faster. As such, these models may generate protein structure predictions that could guide more-efficient structure-based drug design.

## Protein docking and in silico design

DiffDock is a diffusion generative model (DGM) that frames molecular docking as a generative modeling problem. As mentioned in the previous section, a DGM generates new data by passing the sampled noise through the learned denoising neural network. Here, DiffDock completes the diffusion process for ligand translations, ligand rotations and changes to torsion angles. Next, the score and confidence models, the architectures of which are based on SE(3)-equivariant convolutional neural networks over point clouds, predict coordinates and rank the binding poses to select a DiffDock top-1, the prediction with the highest confidence score (Fig. 6a). Alongside the top-1 prediction, DiffDock also provides diverse predictions of likely poses as highlighted by its generation of top-5 and top-10 predictions. The authors trained their model on more than 17,000 protein-ligand PDBbind complexes. DiffDock accuracy outperformed current state-of-the-art search-based, commercial and deep learning methods on top-1 prediction success rate for 40 binding poses. For drug-discovery applications, such as virtual screening or reverse screening of protein targets, accurate high-speed performance is critical. Experimental data from common MOA approaches, such as resistance screening and affinity chromatography, that suggest a protein target can be further supported (or challenged) in silico using DiffDock.

Diffusion models have also recently been adapted for proteindesign tasks. However, these initial efforts face challenges in generating sequences that fold into the predicted structure as well as empirical validation of protein synthesizability or function. Leveraging DGMs and building upon RoseTTAFold, RoseTTAFold diffusion (RFdiffusion) is a generative model capable of designing functional proteins based on explicit specifications. Here, the structure-prediction network of RoseTTAFold is fine-tuned to become the denoising network of a diffusion model. Whereas RoseTTAFold requires an input amino acid sequence, RFdiffusion receives diffused backbone frames (coordinates and orientations). To generate proteins with specific conditions, such as symmetry, binding targets, functional motifs or symmetric functional motifs, the model receives auxiliary conditioning information. Given these inputs, the model denoises the backbone coordinates and predicts a synthesizable protein structure. Here, the authors trained RFdiffusion on diverse datasets including the PDB and AF2 structural models. Most notably, RFdiffusion is an experimentally validated generative model capable of designing an array of proteins that satisfy different properties. Indeed, experimental validation showed that RFdiffusion can generate soluble proteins with higher-order architectures with any desired symmetry.

## Transfer learning in the context of MOA elucidation

As discussed in the previous section, transfer learning is a powerful method for addressing data-limited tasks, such as modeling omics networks. These networks require extensive data to accurately capture how cells respond across different contexts, for example in disease or upon chemical perturbation. Scarcity in these datasets has been remedied by the expansion of technologies such as bulk and single-cell RNA sequencing. However, the connectivity of biological regulatory networks is highly context dependent, making transfer learning ideal for modeling sample-limited biological contexts (for example, limited patient samples in clinical settings).

Geneformer is an attention-based model with six transformer encoder units that uses transfer learning to enable context-aware predictions (gene dosage sensitivity, for example) from tissue-specific single-cell  transcriptomes.  The  model  was  pretrained  on Genecorpus-30M, a database consisting of approximately 29.9 million single-cell transcriptomes across a wide range of tissues arranged into row (feature) and column (cell) attributes. The feature attributes include Ensembl annotations for gene ID, name and type (for example, microRNA, mitochondrial or protein coding). The cell attributes include a unique Genecorpus-30M ID, principal organ, specific organ(s) and calculated measurements for each cell (for example, total number of read counts and percentage of mitochondrial reads). During pretraining, each single-cell transcriptome is a rank value encoding of its genes, where genes are ranked based on their expression in the cell and normalized by their expression across Genecorpus-30M. This method deprioritizes abundantly expressed housekeeping genes, as these are normalized to a lower rank; by contrast, genes that have a stronger influence on cell state, such as transcription factors, which may be expressed at lower levels, are moved to a higher rank. The model is then fine-tuned for a specific task. The authors applied Geneformer to predict chromatin dynamics, gene dosage and hierarchy in gene networks for identifying therapeutic candidates. In one instance, a limited number of patient single-cell transcriptomic samples from non-failing hearts or hearts affected by hypertrophic or dilated cardiomyopathy were used to fine-tune Geneformer. To validate the model's performance, the authors inhibited Geneformer-predicted targets in an in vitro cardiomyopathy disease model, resulting in improved cardiac tissue. In the context of MOA elucidation, Geneformer may be an effective tool for predicting a drug target given single-cell transcriptomes of healthy and disease state samples.

## Step 3: translational investigations

In the later stages of preclinical drug discovery, a primary objective is to optimize promising molecules into more-viable drug candidates. Conventionally, hit-to-lead optimization consists of detailed structure-activity relationship investigations in which core substructures of the priority compound are modified, and resulting changes in bioactivity are evaluated. These structural modifications enable the optimization of properties such as solubility, ADMET profile (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion and toxicity), potency and target specificity. These properties are not only considered during initial lead optimization but are carefully monitored throughout late preclinical development.

ML algorithms can be implemented to predict drug-like properties and toxicity of molecules, allowing preclinical investigations to better position drug candidates for success in clinical trials. Indeed, an ML-guided multi-property optimization scheme may be ideal to consider clinical properties early in the discovery process, rather than as a means to optimize a drug candidate selected based on bioactivity. Applying a suite of ML models to create overlapping predictions on desired bioactivity, toxicity and other drug-like properties may allow researchers to prioritize favorable clinical properties during hit selection, despite the associated molecules not necessarily being the most potent against the desired target.

## Solubility prediction

From the 1990s to 2017, the proportion of drug candidates that failed clinical trials due to poor drug-like properties decreased from 30-40% to 10-15%. This can be attributed to the identification of chemical properties that correlate with favorable pharmacokinetic behavior, namely, Lipinski's rule of five. One of Lipinski's rules addresses lipophilicity, which can be quantified by log( P ). log( P ) is an important determinant of membrane permeability, solubility, bioavailability and metabolic stability. Experimental methods for measuring log( P ), including shake flask slow-stir methods, are generally time consuming and labor intensive. clog( P ) is a computational method to estimate log( P ) where defined constants for molecular fragments are summed and substituted with correction factors for intermolecular interactions. clog( P ) has been the most commonly used metric for lipophilicity estimation since the 1980s; however, it faces limitations with certain structural features and contains systematic errors between similar molecules 92 . In the context of drug discovery, these small systematic errors may determine whether a compound undergoes further investigation, highlighting the importance of accurate log( P ) computation. To overcome these challenges, ML methods have emerged to increase the accuracy of log( P ) predictions.

A recent work trained four ML architectures (support vector machine (SVM), multilayer perceptron (MLP), XGBoost and RoseTTAFold) to predict log( P ) and log( D ) (distribution coefficient) values of molecules based on molecular descriptors and liquid chromatography retention time, which is correlated with log( P ) and log( D ). Models were trained on 2,070 compounds from the METLIN small-molecule retention time database, for which log( P ) values were available from the ChEMBL database. Molecules were represented as 125-feature RDKit vectors, with liquid chromatography retention time concatenated as an additional feature. The MLP was the best-performing model based on mean absolute error. When compared to existing models for predicting log( P ) values that do not include retention time as a feature, the MLP reduced error rates by 20-30%. However, although including retention time leads to improved performance, appending this feature may not be widely applicable, as it requires collection of experimental data.

## Toxicity prediction

Toxicity is responsible for 30% of drug candidates that fail clinical trials, with the majority attributed to human ether-à-go-go-related gene (hERG) binding and related cardiovascular toxicity. In a 2023 paper, investigators trained an array of ML algorithms to predict hERG toxicity. The authors collected a dataset containing 4,556 compounds and their hERG channel IC50 values determined using the patch-clamp technique. The data were binarized using dual thresholds, where compounds with an IC 50 value below 1 μM were considered hERG blockers and compounds with IC50 values above 10 μM were considered non-blockers. A k -nearest neighbor algorithm, SVM, RoseTTAFold and MLP were trained on four different molecular representations: Morgan fingerprints, AtomParis fingerprints, MACCS keys and TopoTorsion fingerprints. The authors also trained a graph convolutional neural network where features are learned from the graph representation of each molecule. The graph convolutional neural network was the best-performing model based on area under the receiver operator curve 99 . Although these models may be useful in assessing hERG blocking in early stages of drug discovery, the authors did not experimentally validate the predictive ability of their models with novel compounds.

In 2023, ADMET-AI was released: an interactive ML platform for predicting ADMET properties of molecules and comparing them to those of approved drugs.  The authors trained 2 multi-task Chemprop-RDKit models on 41 ADMET datasets from the Therapeutics Data Commons (TDC): 1 regression model trained on the 10 TDC regression datasets and 1 classification model trained on the 31 classification datasets. ADMET-AI has been released as a web interface where users can input SMILES and receive predictions on solubility, oral bioavailability, toxicity and hERG safety, among others. ADMET-AI also compares the inputted molecules with reference drugs from DrugBank, based on the percentile of DrugBank molecules in which the inputted molecules reside for each predicted property, to aid users in interpreting model predictions. This accessible web interface allows users from noncomputational backgrounds to easily leverage ML. Importantly, ADMET-AI is ranked highest among ADMET property predictors that were trained on ADMET datasets from the TDC.

ADMET-AI is uniquely positioned to quickly calculate a large panel of ADMET properties for multi-property compound prioritization. However, for a more targeted analysis concentrating on a select few ADMET properties, it is advisable to conduct an in-depth review of the existing models featured on the TDC leaderboard. Several of these models exhibit superior performance over ADMET-AI when assessed on these individual tasks.

With ethical constraints limiting the mass acquisition of clinical toxicity data, a major challenge in predicting toxicity is modeling the multifaceted relationship between in vitro, in vivo and clinical toxicity data. A recent paper addressed this challenge by training FFNNs on in vitro, in vivo and clinical toxicity data. The authors collected in vitro toxicity data from the Tox21Challenge, in vivo toxicity data from the Registry of Toxic Effects of Chemical Substances dataset and clinical toxicity data from the ClinTox dataset from MoleculeNet. Molecules were represented as Morgan fingerprints or pretrained SMILES embeddings, extracted from a gated recurrent unit-autoencoder trained to translate noncanonical SMILES to canonical SMILES. By using the latent embeddings as input, the authors capture the most important features of each molecule. Multi-task FFNNs trained on SMILES embeddings displayed higher performance than single-task models and existing models from MoleculeNet. Importantly, the researchers used a contrastive explainability approach to identify 'toxicophores', the smallest structural units influencing model classification, from training compounds, with many substructures matching known toxicophores, indicating the model's efficacy in relevant feature learning. However, some identified elements are merely single atoms such as carbon or oxygen, which by themselves are nontoxic.

## Outlook

ML is not a panacea. However, when leveraged appropriately, these methods can enhance our ability to discover, understand and use new medicines. Modern drug-discovery efforts have accumulated an abundance of data, powerful computing infrastructure and public and private partnerships to develop open-source chemical biology datasets as well as accessible ways to test novel compounds at reasonable scale. As ML is widely applicable to a vast array of data types, chemical spaces and biological systems, an ML-guided drug-discovery approach can augment human ability to solve complex problems at the interface of chemistry, biology and disease. In addition to the discussed application of ML to small-molecule drug discovery in this Perspective, ML models have also been applied to biologic therapies and natural products.

With the goal of widespread adoption of ML approaches to accelerate drug discovery, it is imperative that we continue to develop and maintain centralized, quality-controlled repositories of datasets on which robust models can be trained and validated. These repositories must accommodate the variability inherent in data from different pharmaceutical settings and experimental systems and address the challenges posed by datasets that explore unique chemical spaces. Thus, collaborative efforts to develop experimentally transparent, open-source and chemically relatable (to some extent) data resources are needed to enhance the generalizability of models between discrete datasets.

Furthermore, enhancing the interpretability of models is crucial for providing insight into their predictive processes and confirming their ability to discern chemically and biologically important features, rather than exploiting spurious correlations within the data. From a regulatory standpoint, the need for model interpretability is growing, as new regulations demand clear justifications for ML model outputs, particularly in clinical trials. Furthermore, the sole use of in silico benchmarks (clog( P ), QED, uniqueness and validity, for example) to evaluate ML-predicted compounds has important limitations, emphasizing the crucial need for wet laboratory evaluation of outputted molecules. Indeed, encouraging collaboration between data scientists for model development and experimentalists for testing molecules in the laboratory is imperative. This collaboration not only refines existing models with additional data (active learning) but also creates a diverse set of robust models that can be leveraged across domains.

Fortunately, efforts such as this are emerging; notably, the Critical Assessment of Computational Hit-finding Experiments contest invites practitioners to discover new ligands for a range of biologically relevant targets. Subsequently, putative hits undergo thorough in vitro validation, with all pertinent data released as open source, facilitating the advancement of further modeling efforts.

Importantly, we want to note that there are many open-source ML libraries available, which lower the barrier for non-experts in ML to begin benefiting from these approaches, with Chemprop being one common example. Accompanying these resources is the opportunity for chemical biologists to become 'multilingual': proficient in their native area of research as well as in the appropriate application of ML to augment their ongoing efforts. Similarly, the opportunity exists for data scientists to address important drug-discovery problems by acquiring fundamental chemical biology knowledge. Collectively, this will decrease the barriers to effective collaboration between investigators from these two discrete fields of study, resulting in more rapid discovery and development of next-generation medicines.
